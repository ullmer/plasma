Jacopo Sansovino (born Jacopo Tatti, 1486–1570) was a prominent Italian sculptor and architect of the Renaissance, particularly influential in Venice. Originally from Florence, he trained under Andrea Sansovino, whose name he adopted out of admiration 1.

Key Highlights of Sansovino's Life and Work:
Early Career: He began his artistic journey in Florence and later moved to Rome, where he studied classical antiquity and worked on various sculptures, including a statue of St. James the Elder and a Bacchus now housed in the Bargello Museum 2.

Move to Venice: After the Sack of Rome in 1527, Sansovino fled to Venice. There, he was appointed Protomaestro (chief architect) to the Procurators of San Marco, a prestigious position that allowed him to shape much of the city's architectural identity 1.

Major Works in Venice:

Biblioteca Marciana (Library of St. Mark’s): Considered one of the most important Renaissance buildings, praised by Andrea Palladio as the best building since antiquity.
Zecca (Mint): Known for its fortress-like rustication.
Loggetta at the base of the Campanile: A richly decorated structure with sculptures and reliefs.
Various churches: Including San Zulian, San Francesco della Vigna, and Santo Spirito in Isola.
Style and Influence: Sansovino brought the High Renaissance style to Venice, blending classical Roman elements with Venetian traditions. His work is characterized by harmony, proportion, and decorative richness.

Collaborations and Legacy: He was a contemporary and friend of Titian and Pietro Aretino, and his architectural vision helped shape the urban landscape of Venice for generations 2.

The Vestibule and Ceiling Design
The vestibule is particularly admired for its rich paneled ceiling, which is likely what Figure 88 in Ward’s book illustrates. This ceiling is a fine example of Renaissance woodwork, featuring geometric and floral motifs arranged in a coffered pattern. The craftsmanship reflects the influence of classical Roman architecture, which was being revived and reinterpreted during the Renaissance.

Sansovino’s Connection
While Jacopo Sansovino himself may not have directly worked on this vestibule, his style and influence were part of the broader Florentine and Venetian Renaissance movements. His sculptural and architectural vocabulary—marked by classical harmony, decorative richness, and structural clarity—resonates with the aesthetic seen in the vestibule’s ceiling.

The paneled ceiling of the vestibule of the Sacristy of Santo Spirito in Florence was most likely designed by Simone del Pollaiuolo, also known as Il Cronaca. He was responsible for constructing the vestibule between 1492 and 1495, based on a design by Giuliano da Sangallo, who also designed the sacristy itself 1.

While Giuliano provided the architectural vision, it was Il Cronaca who executed the vestibule’s construction, including its richly detailed paneled ceiling. This ceiling is admired for its geometric precision and Renaissance ornamentation, aligning with the classical ideals that both architects embraced.
